Senior Engineering Director · Microsoft

Basharat Wani بشارت وانی

Basharat Wani is back at Microsoft as a Senior Engineering Director in the CISO security organization, based in the Seattle area, building and protecting Microsoft's Crown Jewels — working across security, red team and blue team, and sovereign cloud. He is a builder: his career runs across every major cloud, from the cloud platform at Rackspace in Blacksburg, Virginia, to data center capacity planning at AWS, where his One-Click allocation service lifted accuracy from 23% to 64% across ~800 data centers.

He leads the way he builds: with numbers. He has scaled software engineering organizations multiple times, from 2 people to more than 100, and hired them himself — more than 800 interviews, choosing top engineering talent with care. At AWS he moved 62 of 71 pipelines to CI/CD and cut tickets from 200+ to 25 a week; with his team he launched a new service in six weeks. His teams run on empowerment, psychological safety, and operational excellence, and the record is one of impactful delivery and accelerated promotions.

The part of the story he tells most honestly is the failure. He is a multiple-time failed entrepreneur, and he treats those ventures as the education that made him a better leader: they taught him what scale actually costs, why hiring well matters, and why people must be safe to say what's failing before it fails loudly. That is why psychological safety isn't a slogan in his teams — it's the operating system. He keeps learning, keeps driving diversity and inclusion across the organizations he leads, and keeps his focus where the industry is heading: customer-centric approaches, digital transformation, distributed systems, data, and AI/ML platforms. Home is Bothell, Washington.

Microsoft

Basharat's current role is Senior Engineering Director at Microsoft, based in the Seattle area. He works in Microsoft's CISO security organization, where his work centers on building and protecting Microsoft's Crown Jewels — the company's most critical assets.

This role is a boomerang back to Microsoft — Basharat worked at Microsoft earlier in his career and has now returned. It is the latest chapter of a career spent building and leading the platform and cloud systems that sit underneath products used at scale.

Senior ManagerAmazon Web Services

AWS / Amazon

Amazon Web Services — Senior Manager

At AWS / Amazon, Basharat was a Senior Manager working on the part of the cloud nobody sees but everybody depends on: data center capacity planning — the system that makes “infinite resources on demand” actually true. He set the vision with customers and published a three-year roadmap. Then he built the One-Click Datacenter Resources Allocation Service, a single source of truth that replaced legacy systems and lifted allocation accuracy from 23% to 64% across ~800 data centers. With the team, he also launched the equipment master service as a new AWS service in six weeks.

The same thinking applied to how they worked: 62 of 71 pipelines (87%) moved to CI/CD, and root-cause fixes cut tickets from 200+ to 25 a week. As the org grew, he doubled the team multiple times across geographies — coaching engineers, TPMs, PMs, and managers so their growth tracked the business's biggest work.

From July 2011Blacksburg, Virginia

Director of Software Development, Cloud Apps

Rackspace — cloud computing

Basharat was Director of Software Development, Cloud Apps at Rackspace from July 2011, having joined the Cloud Apps group earlier that year as Head of Product Development, Cloud Apps (February–July 2011).

He led software development at Rackspace's Blacksburg, Virginia location, a site of roughly 80 engineers. He is best documented there as a champion of the internal hackathon: the Blacksburg facility ran a 24-hour hackathon about every six months, with around half the engineering staff taking part — and, unusually, outsiders were welcome to join in.

  • Ran the site's recurring 24-hour internal hackathons, open to outside participants, built on open-source software — so intellectual-property questions never got in the way.
  • Argued for longer formats: overnight sessions produced working applications, where an eight-hour sprint yielded only concepts and wireframes.
  • Credited the program with solutions, new technologies, and proofs of concept that made it back into real work — and with a more engaged, more productive engineering culture.

Documented in Computerworld's feature “Hackathons for the rest of us” — see In Print below.

“Hackathons for the rest of us”

Computerworld's survey of how companies build hackathons into their culture quotes Basharat throughout as the voice of Rackspace's Blacksburg site — on why engineers need room to build the ideas that sit behind the day job, what different formats actually produce, and why the whole thing only works if it stays cultural rather than official. Four of his lines from the piece:

They work 8 to 5 on their assignments backlog, but in the back of their heads they have great ideas that they never have time to work on.

— Basharat Wani, Director of Software Development, Rackspace (Blacksburg, Va.)
